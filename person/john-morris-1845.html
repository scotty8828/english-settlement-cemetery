Biography

John Morris was born 17 June 1845, a son of Richard Morris (1810–1871) and Jane Harrison Morris, both already documented on this site. He was one of at least six children: Edward Morris (1831–1912), Pvt. James Morris (1837–1863), William S. Morris (1842–1891, not in the cemetery's own record), Benjamin Morris (1840–1915, already documented on this site), and Inskip Morris (1849–1930, page pending).

John married Saraph Fish (1859–1895), already documented on this site, a daughter of Ira W. Fish of Rome Township, around 1875–78. Together they had two children, Queen Victoria "Queena" Morris Morris (1879–1951) and Floyd Ira Morris (1888–1954), neither buried at English Settlement Cemetery. Saraph died 31 August 1895 while the children were still growing up. John did not remarry for some years afterward.

Saraph was buried beneath a monument unlike any other in the cemetery: carved in the shape of a tree trunk, a striking and unusual choice, and a real departure in both cost and craftsmanship from the simpler markers around it. No document records who commissioned it, but it is documented at length on her own page, already on this site. A monument of this kind, individually carved rather than ordered off a standard pattern, would have been a deliberate and significant expense for a young farming family — and it's hard not to read it as a measure of the depth of John's grief, and of a more sentimental side to a man otherwise remembered mostly for hard work, wheat, and lumber.

By 1880, census records place John Morris, age 35, in Rome Township with his wife Sarah (Saraph), 21, and an infant daughter, age 1. His occupation is recorded as "lumber manufacturer" — consistent with a family account that after the Civil War, John ran a joint lumber and farming business with his brother Benjamin Morris. When the two eventually divided their holdings, Benjamin took the farm and John kept the lumber business.

A family memoir written by Raymond Foley, a descendant of Benjamin Morris, recounts a story from this period: John planted forty acres of wheat among the stumps on the south end of the farm, left over from clearing the original stand of virgin pine timber — stumps too massive to remove quickly, so the early farmers of Rome Township simply planted around them, sometimes for years, until they eventually rotted out or were cleared later. It turned out to be an unusually good year, and by harvest time the wheat had grown thick enough to hide the stumps entirely. The crop threshed out to more than 2,800 bushels. A local thresherman, Steve Jude, had been bragging around the county that he would thresh for free for anyone who could grow more grain in a day than he could handle — and after some badgering, John held him to it.

Some years after Saraph's death, John married Elizabeth "Libbie" Conover (1867–1937), not yet documented on this site. Soon after, a son was born — the same memoir records that John's sister, meeting him sometime afterward, asked what they had named the baby. His reply: "Gawd, Edny, I don't know. I never inquired." The son was named Burdett.

John continued in the lumber business for some years, and later held a farm on the Warren road, with a house and barn built by an earlier owner's family. According to the family memoir he eventually lost the farm to a man named Dan Reuting, under circumstances the memoir's writer did not fully understand. Other stories recorded in the same memoir add color to his later years: forgetting a horse and buggy left in Spartansburg after a day trip to Corry by train, and a dog-power butter churn that got away from him one morning, spraying cream in every direction when the pressure built too high.

John Morris died 8 June 1928, aged 82. The Foley memoir recalls the writer's last visit with him at what is remembered as his 85th birthday party — a detail that does not quite square with his documented birth and death years, and is noted here rather than silently corrected, consistent with how family recollections are handled elsewhere on this site. Additional biography, including monument details and a death record, will be added as research continues. If you have information about John Morris, please use the contribution form below.

Family memoir — "Grandpa and Uncle John Morris," written by Raymond Foley

"Uncle John raised a big crop of wheat on the south end of the farm at this time. He planted forty acres among the stumps... By harvest time the wheat completely hid the stumps in the field. When it was threshed, there was over twenty-eight hundred bushels."

— Typed family memoir, courtesy Bob Wheatall. Family oral history, written decades after the events described; not independently corroborated by documentary sources.

The wheat-among-the-stumps story is more than a family anecdote — it's a small window into how Rome Township's early farmers actually worked the land. Virgin pine stumps were often too massive to pull or burn out quickly, so fields were planted around them for years, sometimes decades, until the stumps eventually rotted or were cleared later. John Morris's forty acres, still dotted with stumps by the late 1870s or 1880s, illustrates that transitional period between cleared timberland and true farmland that defined much of the settlement's early agricultural history.
