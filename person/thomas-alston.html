Biography

Thomas Alston was born 12 May 1796 in England — a date confirmed by both an independent Ancestry.com record and the precise age carved on his own headstone. His parents were Thomas Alston Sr. (1748–1822) and Ann Inskip (1753–1830). Ann was the sister of Jane Inskip Harrison, matriarch of the family that founded the English Settlement — making Thomas her nephew, and tying the Alston family to the Harrisons by blood a full generation before Thomas's own daughter would marry into the Crossley line, another branch of that same extended family. He arrived in the Titusville area in 1841, an Englishman who settled first in the English Settlement, Rome Township, before eventually establishing himself in Titusville proper. By trade he was a cabinet maker, but in later life he lived comfortably, owning several business properties and lots on North Franklin Street in Titusville.

Thomas married Elizabeth Warrick. His obituary states plainly that she died "last year" — meaning 1877, the year before his own death — which is treated here as the most reliable figure, since it comes from a contemporary account by people who knew him. Other sources give different years: Find A Grave records 1878, and a separate Ancestry hint suggests 1893; both are treated as less reliable than the obituary's firsthand account. Thomas and Elizabeth had at least two children: Jane, who married David Crossley, and Ambrose Alston.

After his wife's death, Thomas lived alone in his small home with no companion but a rat-and-tan dog, to which he was affectionately attached. Neighbors and the staff of the local newspaper described him as a familiar, solitary fixture — smoking his clay pipe at his front gate most fair days, seen on the street corner by print-shop workers heading home at four in the morning. He ate almost nothing but bread, avoided meat entirely, and was never known to wear an overcoat even in the hardest winters — except once, briefly, after buying a second-hand one he wore only a single time. Despite this austerity, he wasn't a miser: he was known to indulge in Little Neck clams at a dollar a dozen when they first appeared each season, and prided himself on paying his taxes promptly and settling every purchase in cash, "hating debt and credit."

Thomas died 25 September 1878, at over 80 years old, from what his obituary called "pure exhaustion of nature" — he had declined medical attention, nursing, or companionship through his final illness. One person stayed close to him through it: his son-in-law David Crossley, husband of his late daughter Jane, who had died nine years earlier in 1869. The obituary records that Crossley "was a daily visitor during the lonely old man's last sickness, and being an old friend, has attended to his affairs since that event" — a quiet, lasting loyalty that outlasted the marriage that first connected the two families.

Obituary — "Death of a Singular Old Gentleman," 1878
Titusville Herald, 28 September 1878, page 3.

The late Thomas Alston, whose funeral will take place to-day, was one of our oldest citizens and had lived here since 1841. He was an Englishman and used to live in the English Settlement, Rome township. He was a cabinet maker by trade, but for many years had lived at ease, enjoying an income sufficient for his moderate wants, being the owner of several good paying business houses and lots on North Franklin Street. Since the death of his wife, last year, he had lived alone in his little home, with no companion but his rat-and-tan dog, to whom he was affectionately attached.

The old man might be seen almost any fair day, at his front gate, in his shirt sleeves, bare headed, smoking his clay pipe, and looking meditatively out in the street. The compositors of the Herald office, in going home summer mornings at about 4 o'clock, almost invariably saw the old man on the street corner. "Solitary and alone." His table was as simple as a hermit's; he eschewed animal food and lived almost literally on bread alone; yet he was never sick and died from pure exhaustion of nature at over 80, declining medical attention, or nursing, or any companionship.

He was not known ever to indulge in the luxury of an overcoat, even in the hardest winter, except when he lately bought a second-hand one, and that he never was known to wear but once. Yet he did not seem to be parsimonious. He would have Little Neck clams on their first appearance in the market, though they cost a dollar a dozen, and seemed to enjoy this species of extravagance. He was the first man in the city to pay his taxes and assessments every year and liked to pay in cash for all he bought, hating debt and credit.

Crossley was a daily visitor during the lonely old man's last sickness, and being an old friend, has attended to his affairs since that event.

Transcribed and shared on Find A Grave by contributor Virginia Bates-Hulings. Spelling and punctuation lightly regularized for readability.

Confirmed connection to the Harrison family: Thomas Alston's parents are Thomas Alston Sr. and Ann Inskip, confirmed through matching wife, children, and dates verified across independent records. Ann Inskip was the sister of Jane Inskip Harrison — making Thomas Alston her nephew, and connecting the Alston family to the Harrisons by blood, independent of Jane Alston's later marriage into the Crossley family. David Crossley, Jane Alston's husband, was Jane Inskip Harrison's own grandson through her daughter Anne — meaning David and Jane Alston Crossley were second cousins to each other, both descended from the two Inskip sisters, Jane and Ann. This resolves what had been a promising but unconfirmed lead, independently suggested by both an Ancestry hint and Craig Thornber's separate genealogical research.
