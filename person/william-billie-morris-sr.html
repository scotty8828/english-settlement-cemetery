Biography

William Morris was born in Lancashire, England, in 1811, son of James Morris (1782–1858) — the Lancashire blacksmith whose 1819 pikehead conviction and eventual emigration is the centerpiece biography already documented on this site — and Ann Azlean Morris (1790–1863). He crossed to America as a boy; two contemporary obituaries give conflicting details of exactly when, one dating the crossing to 1827 with his parents, the other stating he was twelve years old at the time. Both agree the family landed in New York.

William was a younger brother to Richard Morris, already documented on this site as one of the three men who walked to found the English Settlement in Rome Township in 1833. Of James and Ann Azlean Morris's ten children, few lived to old age; by the time of William's own death in 1890, only their brother Robert Morris, of Tidioute, Pennsylvania, remained alive.

William learned the machinist's trade and worked it in Pittsburgh, at Irvineton, and at Colonel Roberts' machine shop. In 1848 he bought a hundred-acre, partly-cleared farm at East Rome Township known as the Vroman farm, agreeing to pay a dollar an acre, and later added ninety more acres at a dollar and a quarter. He paid for the land with his machinist's wages, working away from home for long stretches while his eldest son, William Jr., looked after the farm and family in his place. Following what his own obituary called English custom, William gave each son who stayed with him until the age of twenty-one a portion of his farm.

William married Jane Harrison — already documented at length elsewhere on this site — and the couple raised at least eight children together at East Rome Township. Jane died in 1885. William Morris died five years later, on the morning of 5 August 1890, at seventy-eight or seventy-nine years old; his obituaries agree he had never been seriously ill in his life and died simply of old age, without pain or struggle. His funeral was held the following Wednesday at ten o'clock, Rev. Mr. Bates officiating, and he was buried in the English graveyard — on what had once been the Benjamin Harrison farm — the same cemetery already holding three generations of his family. He was survived by four sons, William Jr., Benjamin, James, and John B., and two daughters, Mrs. John Meyers and Mrs. Thomas Sedden.

In September 1887, William Morris was accused of an offense against his six-year-old niece, Carrie Harrison, daughter of Benjamin Harrison. The case was tried before a justice of the peace at Spartansburg on 29 September 1887, on a warrant sworn out by Calvin Johnson of Bear Lake, with whom the child had been staying in the weeks before her death. Carrie's mother testified that she and her husband had gone out to milk cows on the evening of 28 July, leaving the child at the Morris home; on returning home, Carrie said the old man had hurt her. Mrs. Harrison neither examined the child herself nor told her husband. Three days later Carrie was sent to Bear Lake, into the care of a family named Woodward; it was there that blood was first noticed on her bed and clothing, and a physician who examined her found she had been assaulted. Carrie died at Bear Lake on 12 August.

At trial, Benjamin Harrison himself testified that he had not even been home on the day in question — he had left the Sunday before and did not return until the following Saturday — directly contradicting his wife's account of the evening. Another relative testified the child showed no sign of distress when she left home, and no fear of passing the Morris house afterward, which the justice noted would have been unlikely had the accusation been true. Finding no credible evidence connecting William to the child's death, the justice of the peace pronounced the story "absurd, and a black-mailing scheme," and discharged William as innocent. The child's death from mistreatment was not in dispute; who was actually responsible was never established.

A note on this account. This entry was not originally planned for William's biography. It is included here, in full, because the bare accusation — without any mention of the trial or its outcome — has circulated for years on unsourced Ancestry.com family trees. Repeating a 130-year-old accusation while omitting its acquittal is irresponsible and sensationalized treatment of a matter that involved a real child's death and a man's name. Once that incomplete version was already public, the only responsible response was to go get the whole record and tell the whole story. The author traveled to the Crawford County Historical Society in Meadville to retrieve the complete trial coverage; it is presented below in full, exactly as published.

The 1887 accusation & acquittal — full record
Titusville Herald · 29 September 1887 · "Acquitted — No Evidence Against William Morris, Sr. — Senseless Gossip"
"Spartansburg, Sept. 29. — [Special.] — Wm. Morris, Sr., of the English Settlement, was tried at Spartansburg to-day on a charge of outrage on the person of Carrie Harrison, aged 6, the warrant for his arrest being sworn out by Calvin Johnson, of Bear Lake, with whom the child stopped before her death. F. B. Guthrie, of this city, represented the defendant and District Attorney Higgins, of Warren county, the plaintiff. The outrage is alleged to have occurred on July 28th. Mrs. Harrison swore she and her husband, Benjamin, went out on that evening to milk cows. Carrie went to Morris's house and stayed there until three cows had been milked. On returning home the child said the old man had placed her on a lounge and hurt her. Mrs. Harrison didn't say anything to her husband, nor did she examine the child. Three days afterward she sent the child to Bear Lake in charge of a young man named Johnson, aged 18, where Carrie stayed with a colored family named Woodward. On that morning Mr. Harrison claims to have noticed signs of blood on the child's bed. Mrs. Woodward testified that she was not at home when Carrie arrived there, Aug. 1. When she came home Carrie complained of not feeling well, and looked ill. After the child had been there a few days Mrs. Woodward noticed blood on the bed and on the child's garments. She remained with them until the 12th of August. She was then taken to Cal. Johnson's, where she died. Johnson swore that two or three days after the child had been with them she said she was sick and that the "old man," mentioning no particular name, had injured her as above described. A physician was then summoned, and on examination said she had been tampered with. Mr. Benjamin Harrison testified he was not at home on July 28. He had gone away the Sunday previous, and did not return until the Saturday following, thus giving the lie direct to his wife's evidence. When he did return he found the child all right and went away again without hearing anything of the story. Another relative testified there was apparently nothing wrong with the child when she left. The facts that the mother made no examination and told her husband nothing about the matter; that the child was apparently well for several days before leaving home, which in case of outrage on a child of six, would not have been probable; that she exhibited no fright or aversion to pass by Morris' house after the evening of the alleged occurrence, which, had it been true, would probably have given her a terror of that vicinity; the fact that no one could state anything positively beyond what little the child herself had said, led the justice to pronounce the story absurd and a black-mailing scheme, and he discharged Mr. Morris as innocent. Mr. Morris's innocence being established, the fact still remains that the child died from shameful treatment at the hands of some one — who, will probably never be known. The child is dead, no one seems to know much about the matter, and the circumstantial evidence, so far as it related to Morris, was nothing, and the guilty party, even were he arrested, would probably never be convicted on the evidence brought against him."
Obituaries
Crawford Journal · 14 August 1890 · p. 2
"Died on the morning of the 5th inst. William Morris Sr., aged 79 years; born in Lancashire, England, and came to this country with his parents in 1827, landing in New York; learned the machinist trade. His wife the former Jane Harrison died several years ago and leaves six children. He was of a family of ten children, only one surviving is (his brother) Robert Morris, Tidioute Pa."
Titusville Morning Herald · 7 August 1890 · p. 4 · "Death of an Old Resident"
"Mr. William Morris, Sr, farmer, East Rome Township, aged 79, died Monday at his home. He was born in England and came to this country when 12 years of age, and afterwards followed it in Pittsburg. Mr. William Morris bought a farm of one hundred acres, which was partly cleared, called the Vroman farm, agreeing to pay one dollar an acre and afterwards added 90 acres at $1.25 an acre. This was in 1848. Mr. Morris paid for the farm by working at his trade in Pittsburg, Irvineton and Col. Roberts' machine shop, his son Wm Jr, looked after the farm and family. According to English custom Mr. Morris gave his sons who stayed with him till age 21, portions of his farm. He leaves four sons, William Jr., Benjamin, James, John B., and two daughters — Mrs. John Meyers and Mrs. Thomas Sedden. The deceased was never sick a day in his life, and died of old age, without pain or struggle. The funeral took place at 10 o'clock Wednesday, Rev. Mr. Bates officiating. The remains were placed in the English graveyard, situated on the Benjamin Harrison farm."

Emigration age/year conflict: The Crawford Journal obituary says William came to America "with his parents in 1827." The Titusville Morning Herald obituary independently says he was "12 years of age" at the crossing — which, given his 1811 birth, would place it closer to 1823. The two contemporary obituaries, published days apart, do not agree. Not resolved.
Ten children, most did not survive to old age: The Crawford Journal obituary states James Morris and Ann Azlean Morris had ten children, of whom only Robert Morris (of Tidioute, PA) was still living at William's own death in 1890. Only four of the ten — Richard, William, James (d. 1846), and Robert — are currently identified by name on this site; the other six remain undocumented.
Multiple "James Morris" in this family — do not conflate: This page's James Morris (1813–1846, William's brother, died young) is distinct from both James Morris (1782–1858, the family patriarch) and Pvt. James Morris (1837–1863, son of Richard Morris and Jane Harrison Morris, the Civil War soldier already documented on this site), and also from James "Jimmy" Morris (1848–1921, William's own son, listed among his children above). Four different men named James Morris appear across two generations of this family.
1887 accusation — identity confirmed: The Titusville Herald's account describes the accused as an unmarried man "about 70" years old; this William Morris Sr. was married with eight children and would have been about 76 in 1887. The workplace detail — "old Col. Roberts shops" in the original 1887 Titusville newspaper report of the accusation, matched almost word for word by his 1890 obituary — together with the "of the English Settlement" identification in the acquittal article, confirms this is the same man. Approximate ages given in period newspaper accounts are not considered a reliable basis for disputing identity.
Two different Morris land purchases — do not conflate: The 1848 Vroman farm purchase described here, made by William individually, is a separate and later transaction from the 400-acre, three-lot division made in 1833 by the three founders (Inskip Harrison, Benjamin Inskip Harrison, and Richard Morris), documented on Inskip Harrison's page.
