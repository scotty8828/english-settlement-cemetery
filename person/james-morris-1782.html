Biography

Pike Lowe

James Morris was born 4 August 1782 at Whitefield, Lancashire, twin brother to William Morris — both baptized the same day. Their parents, John Morris (1739–1826) and Anne Rawson, had married two years earlier, on 2 January 1780, at St Mary's Church, Prestwich. John Morris was a shoemaker and farmer who went on to build Pike Lowe Farm in Haslingden Grane, a farming valley in the West Pennine Moors — a house that, family research suggests, included a secret underground chamber from very early on. Haslingden Grane no longer meaningfully exists; the whole valley, once home to well over a thousand people, was cleared in the mid-19th century for reservoir construction, and almost nothing of Pike Lowe remains today but ruins on a hillside.

William stayed at Pike Lowe his whole life, becoming a weaver and farmer there until his death in 1849. James left the family farm and trained as a blacksmith — skilled enough that a legal record from 1820 describes him as "not only a blacksmith but a machine-maker," capable of building the hand-cranked winding mechanisms used in textile weaving. He married a woman recorded as Ann (Aglwen, or Azlean).

Two Pikeheads, October 1819

Two months after the Peterloo Massacre in nearby Manchester, Lancashire's authorities moved swiftly against a wave of underground weapons production feeding the parliamentary reform movement. James was swept up in it. Court records show that on 12 October 1819, he sold two pikeheads at Accrington Grove, telling the buyers it was "no use going to the intended meeting at Bolton without such weapons" — a reform rally planned for the very next day. He was one of several: contemporary newspapers describe a coordinated crackdown across the region in these same days, with dragoons and the Riot Act deployed against at least one other blacksmith less than 48 hours later, and a sworn deposition survives describing an informant posing as a customer to draw out a separate blacksmith's confession of having made "some hundreds of pikes" for buyers as far off as Harrogate.

Family memory, recorded generations later by a descendant, Jane Morris, adds detail no court record does: that James's house was searched twice before the weapons were finally found hidden on a ledge inside an old chimney on the third search, and that he refused even direct questioning about how the weapons were forged — afraid that if his method got out, he'd be "beheaded" for the knowledge. Whatever the precise truth of that last detail, the core of the family's story — a blacksmith, secret weapons, a search of his house, arrest, imprisonment, then emigration — holds up remarkably well against the documented record.

Trial and Lancaster Castle

James was tried on 31 March 1820 — just weeks after the death of George III — before Mr. Justice Bayley at the Lancaster Assizes, alongside a co-defendant, John Knowles. Undefended, he pleaded not guilty, but the evidence was strong: the prosecution had specifically tested whether he could and would supply more pikeheads, and he had. He was convicted and sentenced to twenty months' imprisonment at Lancaster Castle — the same prison already holding several reform-movement prisoners swept up in the aftermath of Peterloo.

One early newspaper report named the convicted man "William Morris, of Pike Law" rather than James — almost certainly a mix-up with his twin, whose entire identity in the community was bound up with that same family farm. But two independent sources, working entirely apart from each other and from this project — the family's own account, and a Lancashire genealogist researching the wider Morris family generations later with no connection to either — agree it was James.

America

According to family memory, once released, James decided the best thing he could do was take his family and start for America, where they could have "a government to their own liking." He settled first in Paterson, New Jersey, just across the river from New York. It was his children's marriages — pulling them toward Rome Township, Pennsylvania, where the Harrison family had already put down roots — that eventually drew James there too, later in life. In America he stayed in his trade: never a farmer, he worked as a blacksmith and mason, employed at the Enterprise works building and repairing furnaces. He lived in Titusville, near what is now the site of the Titusville Trust Company.

Family memory records that late in life, James looked at the country he'd adopted and felt he had found what he'd once wanted England to become: a government of the people's own choosing, without the entangled authority of an established church he had once risked his life to defy.

Legacy

James Morris died 6 September 1858 in Crawford County, Pennsylvania, and was buried at what is now English Settlement Cemetery. His sons Richard, William, James, and Robert carried the family forward into a community already deeply intertwined with the Harrisons — Richard's own marriage to Jane Harrison Morris helped found Rome Township's English Settlement in 1833. Ann joined James in this cemetery five years after his death.

This page reflects everything currently gathered from English sources — court records, contemporary newspapers, family memoir, and independent genealogical research. American-side material, including period obituaries, is still being gathered and will be folded in before this page is considered finished. Also flagged for the next revision: the closing paragraph currently reads as though Richard arrived to an already-established community — it needs to instead reflect that Richard, alongside Jane's brothers Benjamin Inskip and Inskip Harrison, was one of the three families who founded Rome Township's English Settlement in 1833, not a later arrival to it.
