Biography

Richard Morris was born 16 July 1810 in Haslingden, Lancashire, England — the same corner of Lancashire, near Accrington, where his father James Morris's own story unfolded — and was baptized at Bury St Mary Parish Church on 16 September 1810. He was the eldest known son of James Morris and Ann (Aglen) Morris. He grew up alongside at least three younger brothers — William, James, and Robert — in the years before the family made the decision to leave England for America.

Around 1826, Richard crossed the Atlantic with his parents, landing in New York City. The family is believed to have settled for a time across the river in Paterson, New Jersey, before eventually continuing on toward northwestern Pennsylvania. On 28 April 1830, Richard married Jane Harrison — daughter of Benjamin Harrison and Jane Inskip Harrison, the matriarch whose own family would give the settlement much of its shape — at the Second Reformed Church in Wyckoff, New Jersey. A separate account, published on the Civil War letters archive sparedshared23.com, describes Jane as having emigrated separately from Richard and already having spent four years in the United States working as a weaver in the textile mills by the time they married in Paterson, New Jersey — differing in detail from the Wyckoff church record but not necessarily contradicting it. Through this marriage, Richard became connected by blood and by partnership to two of his brothers-in-law, Inskip Harrison and Benjamin Inskip Harrison. Their eldest son, Edward, was born the following January in New York City itself — evidence that the young family was still moving between New York and New Jersey as late as 1831, several years after Richard's parents first landed. Edward would go on to marry Ellen Harrison, daughter of Richard's own brother-in-law Benjamin Inskip Harrison, in 1854.

In 1834, Richard and Jane came to Rome Township, Crawford County, and settled on a piece of wild land, building a log house and making a home. A family memoir records what happened next in plain terms: Richard was one of three English families who settled what became known as the English Settlement. The three heads of these families were named directly — Richard Morris, Inskip Harrison, and Benjamin Harrison. In other words, the settlement's founding wasn't three unrelated households arriving separately; it was Richard, having married into the Harrison family, standing alongside his own wife's two brothers to break the ground together. The full memoir passage is quoted below.

Richard and Jane raised at least six children together: Edward, James, Benjamin, William, John, and Inskip Morris — the youngest carrying forward the Harrison family's own name, a naming pattern already well established across this project. Their son James died in 1863 of wounds received at Gettysburg; his own biography, including a letter home written days after he was wounded, is documented elsewhere on this site.

Richard died 19 May 1871 in Rome Township, at 60 years old, and was buried at English Settlement Cemetery — the ground he himself had helped clear and settle nearly four decades earlier.

A 1946 article in the Titusville Herald preserves one more story about Richard, passed down through only one generation before it was recorded in print. Richard's grandson, David R. Morris, told the paper that his father — Richard's son, Inskip Morris — had told him that Richard helped at the Drake Well while it was being drilled, on the strength of his familiarity with steam engine operation. If accurate, this places Richard Morris at the site of the first commercial oil well in the United States, drilled by Edwin Drake near Titusville in 1859 — a discovery that launched the American oil industry, a few miles from the farm Richard had built with his own hands.

A family memoir — naming the founders of the English Settlement
A handwritten family memoir, shared with the museum by researcher Bob Wheatall, records Richard's story and the founding of the settlement directly.

Richard Morris of Rome Township is a son of James and Anna (Aglen) Morris, and was born in Lancashire, England. He came to America in 1826, landing in New York City. He married Jane Harrison, daughter of Benjamin and Jane (Inskip) Harrison, of New Jersey. They came to Rome Township in 1834, where they settled on a piece of wild land, built a log house, and made a home.

He is one of the three English families that settled what is called the English Settlement. The three heads of these families: Richard Morris, Inskip Harrison, and Benjamin Harrison.

— from a Morris family memoir
Shared with the museum courtesy of Bob Wheatall. This same memoir also names Richard's father, James Morris, and provides the earliest confirmed date for the family's emigration.

Ancestry's indexed baptismal record shows a derived "Birth Date" of 16 July 1809 — one year earlier than the 1810 used consistently elsewhere (Find A Grave, the headstone, and this project's own master record). This is very likely a computed estimate rather than a date actually written in the parish register: the register records Richard's baptism on 16 September 1810 at "age 1," and indexing systems commonly back-calculate an estimated birth year from a stated age at baptism, which can introduce a year of error either direction. The headstone's precise age at death — 60 years, 10 months, 3 days as of 19 May 1871 — only works out correctly for a birth date of 16 July 1810, which is treated as the more reliable figure here. Both the day and month (16 July) agree across every source; only the year is in question.
Conflict to resolve: The family memoir gives 1834 as the year Richard and Jane came to Rome Township. A separate account, published on the Civil War letters archive sparedshared23.com — which also transcribes a substantial set of letters written by their son James during his Civil War service — states the family moved to Rome Township in 1837, with James born there that October rather than earlier. Neither date has been changed to match the other; both are preserved here pending further research. The same source is the origin for the detail that Jane emigrated separately from Richard and had already worked four years as a weaver in American textile mills before their 1830 marriage.
The Drake Well story comes from a 1946 newspaper account, itself reporting oral family history passed from Richard's son Inskip Morris to his own son, David R. Morris, and then to the Titusville Herald — a single generation of transmission before it reached print, which lends it real weight, though it remains family testimony rather than a contemporary 1859 record naming Richard directly. Marked here as a documented family account rather than independently verified fact.
This project's own master record currently lists one of Richard's sons as "John Brown Morris" (1845–1928). The error traces back to Find A Grave itself, which lists him under that name on Richard's own memorial — and since this project's master list was built from Find A Grave records, the mistake carried over directly. Ancestry.com's independent record gives his name simply as John Morris, with no "Brown." Census research already applied elsewhere on this site (see Private James Morris's page) confirms John Morris and John Brown Morris are two different people — cousins, both of whom happened to marry women named Seraph, which is very likely how the two men were conflated on Find A Grave in the first place. The master record entry has not yet been corrected but is flagged here. Jane Harrison Morris (1806–1887), Richard's wife, does not yet have her own biography page on this site.
