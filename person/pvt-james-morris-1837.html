Biography

James was born 10 October 1837 in Rome Township, Crawford County, Pennsylvania, the third child of Richard Morris and Jane Harrison Morris — grandson of James and Ann Morris on his father's side, and of Benjamin and Jane Inskip Harrison on his mother's, tying together the two families who had founded the English Settlement a generation before. He grew up alongside at least five siblings: Edward, Benjamin, William, John, and Inskip Morris — and, per the 1860 census, a younger sister as well, not yet identified by name. His eldest brother, Edward, married Ellen Harrison — daughter of Benjamin Inskip Harrison, one of the settlement's three founders — in 1854.

The family maintained a close relationship with their neighbors David and Jane (Alston) Crossley, documented elsewhere on this site; James's surviving letters address David as "cousin," suggesting an actual family connection between the Morris and Crossley households, not yet independently confirmed. David Crossley personally relayed messages and letters for James during his 1861 service, and the Crossleys' own son Thomas signed up for military service around the same time.

Family lore has it that James enlisted against his father's wishes. In fact, this was James's second enlistment. Immediately after President Lincoln's April 1861 call for volunteers following the fall of Fort Sumter, James joined the "Erie Regiment" — Company F, under Captain Morgan, part of Colonel John W. McLane's 1st Regiment Pennsylvania Volunteers — for a three-month term. He trained at Camp Wilkins near Pittsburgh and later at Camp Wright, Hulton Station, further up the Allegheny River. A series of his letters home to his parents survives from this period, describing camp food shortages, lice, swimming in the river, boxing matches, and the general disorder of a hastily-raised regiment that was never properly armed — issued only obsolete 1817 flintlock muskets — and never called into active combat. The regiment returned to Erie in July 1861 without having fought. James did not re-enlist when the remnants reorganized as the 83rd Pennsylvania Volunteers; he waited fourteen months before enlisting a second time, in September 1862, in Company I of the 150th Pennsylvania Volunteer Infantry — the regiment he would serve with at Gettysburg.

James enlisted as a private in Company I of the 150th Pennsylvania Volunteer Infantry, part of the First Corps of the Army of the Potomac — the regiment better known, alongside its sister units, as one of the "Bucktail" regiments of Pennsylvania. On 1 July 1863, the opening day of the Battle of Gettysburg, the 150th Pennsylvania was thrown into the fighting at the McPherson Barn, in some of the war's fiercest and most costly combat. James was struck by a bullet or buckshot across the top of his scalp. The regiment's own history records him as mortally wounded that day. The First Corps' commanding general, Major General John F. Reynolds, was killed in the same day's fighting nearby — almost certainly the "Corps General" James mentions in his letter home.

Three days later, on 4 July 1863, James wrote to his parents from an old barn where he and a companion named Robert — wounded in the foot by a shell fragment, and whose exact relation to James is not yet known — were sheltering with the wounded. Badly outnumbered for paper, he wrote his letter on a scrap he had found, the Confederates having taken his own stationery. His letter, transcribed in full below, describes a regiment gutted by the fighting: Company I reduced to nine men, and nearly every field officer above him killed or wounded.

James did not recover. He was moved north to the military hospital at Fort Schuyler, New York, where he died of his wounds on 18 July 1863 — seventeen days after he was shot, and just two weeks after writing home to tell his parents not to worry. He was 25 years, 9 months, and 8 days old. Family tradition also holds that his mother, Jane, arranged in secret to have his remains brought home, and had him buried in the cemetery at night. He rests at English Settlement Cemetery, among the family and community he had grown up in.

In his own words — letter home, 4 July 1863
Written three days after being wounded at Gettysburg, on a scrap of paper, from an old barn sheltering the wounded.

Dear Father and Mother, I thought I would put this little note in Robert's. Both of us have got slight wounds at present. We lay in an old barn. I got a small bullet or buck shot through the top of my scalp. Robert got a piece of shell in the side of his foot. We was both wounded in the first day's fight. We had a very hot time amongst the shot and shell and minies. Some like being amongst a swarm of bees. I will send you an account of what I saw of the battle next time I write.

Our regiment suffered heavily. Our Co. had nine men left. Our Corps General was killed, our Brigadier was wounded, one Col. shot in the mouth. Lieut. Col. shot through the right arm, our Major through the body, our Adjutant through the right shoulder. The other shoulder-straps suffered as much as the field officers.

I must conclude for this time. You need not feel anxious about me. I am all right. I have no paper, stamps, or anything else. The rebels got them, so I had to take this piece of old paper that I found.

This from
Jas. Morris
Original letter photographed and transcribed courtesy of descendant Robert Wheatall, via Find A Grave memorial #47673451. Spelling lightly regularized for readability; the letter itself is preserved as a family document.

Conflict to resolve: The sparedshared23.com account states the family moved to Rome Township in 1837, with James born there that October — three years later than the 1834 date given in the family memoir on Richard Morris's own page. Also per this source, Richard and Jane emigrated separately and married in Paterson, New Jersey in 1830, with Jane said to have already spent four years in the U.S. working as a weaver in the textile mills — differing in detail (though not necessarily in conflict) from the Wyckoff, NJ church marriage record already documented on Richard's page. Neither account has been changed to match the other; both are preserved here pending further research.
Birth date (10 October 1837) is calculated backward from the monument's recorded age at death (25 years, 9 months, 8 days as of 18 July 1863) rather than from a birth record — marked Documented because it derives directly from the carved inscription, but a baptismal or census record would be worth finding to confirm it independently. James's brother William Morris (1842–1891) is a different person from "William S. Morris Jr." (1837–1915) elsewhere in this project's records — that William Jr. is a first cousin, son of Richard's brother William Morris Sr. and Jane Harrison Morris, not James's own brother. The two shared a first name a generation apart, which briefly looked like a data error but is not. James's brother is John Morris (1845–1928) — not "John Brown Morris," a different person (a cousin) who Ancestry.com conflates with him. Census records confirm both men are real and distinct; the confusion likely arises because both John and his cousin John Brown Morris married women named Seraph. This is a known, ongoing tangle in the public record that this project is working through case by case; James's page has been corrected accordingly, but the underlying confusion is not yet fixed sitewide. "Robert," James's wounded companion named in the letter, has not been identified — he may or may not be a relative. The story that James enlisted against his father's wishes, and that his mother Jane secretly arranged his nighttime burial at English Settlement, comes from family tradition recalled across several family memoirs — marked Unverified pending a documentary source, but preserved here as part of the family's own memory of him.
