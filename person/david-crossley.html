Biography

David Crossley was born 17 March 1817 in Blackburn, Lancashire, England, the son of Robert Crossley and Anne (also recorded as Ann or Nancy) Harrison Crossley — Anne being a daughter of Jane Inskip Harrison, the matriarch whose family would found the English Settlement a generation later. Family tradition, recorded in his 1880 obituary, held that David was brought to America as a child by his grandmother. Two independently published historical accounts tell a different, more dramatic story: David ran away from home and crossed the Atlantic as a stowaway in 1828, at eleven years old. One working theory holds that both accounts are true at once — that David stowed away aboard the very ship his grandmother's household was sailing on, running from home to join a crossing he wasn't officially part of, which would explain how a later, secondhand telling simplified his journey to "brought by his grandmother." Confirming this would likely require matching a ship's manifest to Jane's own documented household, since stowaways rarely appear on passenger lists themselves. He found relatives at Paterson, New Jersey, and lived with them for roughly seven years before apprenticing himself out to learn blacksmithing in New York, a trade he worked at for about twelve years.

David grew to manhood in New York City. There, on 17 March 1839 — his own twenty-second birthday — he married Jane Alston, daughter of Thomas Alston. In the winter of 1841–42, David walked from New York to Titusville, Pennsylvania, to scout the area for a place to settle, then walked back to New York that spring. That autumn, he brought his family west for good. David and Jane were, in fact, second cousins: both descended from the two Inskip sisters, Jane Inskip Harrison and Ann Inskip Alston, who married the founders of two different branches of this extended family.

The Crossleys tried farming for a few years before David returned to blacksmithing in Titusville. They raised at least ten children, seven of whom died young, most within a devastating span in the winters of 1860 and 1861 — that story, and Jane's own, are documented on her biography page elsewhere on this site. Of the children who survived to adulthood, son James P. Crossley and daughter Jennie S. Crossley Burns lived on well past their parents; a third, Thomas J. Crossley, is also documented on this site, having died in 1875 and leaving two children of his own, Cora and David.

David's "energetic spirit" — in the words of his obituary — found its outlet in the discovery of petroleum. In partnership with William Barnsdall, William H. Abbott, and P. T. Witherop, under the firm name Crossley, Witherop & Company, David helped drill what several historical accounts describe as the world's third oil well, completed 10 March 1860 — just months after Edwin Drake's landmark well had proven the region's oil could be commercially produced. The well, sunk 140 feet using a spring-pole rig, is credited in David's obituary with 200 barrels a day; a more technical 1898 account puts sustained production at 70 to 75 barrels a day. Both figures are likely accurate at different moments — the shallow, coarse sands typical of this earliest phase of the boom often produced a dramatic initial surge that quickly settled into a lower, steadier rate. Production declined that autumn and the well was abandoned, but David wasn't finished: in 1865 he bought out his partners and drilled deeper, to 550 feet, only to abandon it again due to water. In 1872, working alongside his own sons, he tried again, eventually managing the water well enough to turn the property profitable. In 1873, David partnered with William Barnsdall once more to drill the first producing well in the Bradford oil field — a second, separate chapter in the region's oil history. His health failed in 1875, and he retired from the business.

Not every part of David's Titusville life was straightforward. David had purchased his blacksmith shop's land from Jonathan Titus, the town's original proprietor, back in 1855, well before the oil boom transformed the area. As Titusville's population and street grid exploded through the 1860s, the city moved to redesign and open Martin Street directly through ground David had occupied and built on for over a decade. He resisted, refusing to give up an established business and property on the city's say-so, and the dispute went to court: in 1869 David and his son Thomas J. Crossley were convicted of obstructing the street, and a protracted legal fight continued into 1870, eventually drawing in Jane's estate after her death and their daughter Jennie's own claim to the land. Read one way, it's a conviction; read another, it's a longtime property owner standing his ground against a rapidly growing city trying to redraw the map through land he'd held since before the boom that made the city grow.

David was also a committed Freemason. He was initiated into Oil Creek Lodge on 22 December 1856, barely three weeks after the lodge itself was founded, and served as its Master twice, in 1859 and again in 1864.

David fell ill and, after a long decline, died at a quarter before three in the morning of 11 October 1880, at the home of his old friend and business partner William Barnsdall, at 63 years old. He was buried at English Settlement Cemetery with Masonic honors. His detailed will, registered that October, named his son James P. Crossley as executor and trustee, distributed his oil interests and Titusville properties among his surviving children and Thomas's two young children, and named witnesses including his own brother-in-law, Ambrose Alston. The will also reveals that David had served as trustee under the will of his father-in-law, Thomas Alston, appointing his son James to succeed him in that role — a formal record of the same devotion Thomas's own obituary described, when it noted that David had cared for his lonely father-in-law through his final illness and administered his affairs afterward.

Obituary — Titusville Morning Herald, 11 October 1880
Published the day of his death.

Death of David Crossley — At a quarter before 3 o'clock this morning David Crossley, who has been sick for a long time, breathed his last at his home in Hon. William Barnsdall's house. Deceased was 63 years of age and has lived in this section of Pennsylvania for the past thirty-five years, having come to America from his native country, England, at an early age. He leaves one daughter, Mrs. Burns, and one son. The burial will take place next Wednesday and the Masons will take charge of the funeral.

Yesterday morning we were pained to chronicle the death of David Crossley, one of our oldest and most esteemed citizens. His soul passed peacefully away to rest. Mr. Crossley was born in England and when a child was brought to the United States by his grandmother, landing in New York city. In the Metropolis he grew to manhood and married Miss Alston, daughter of Mr. Thomas Alston. Removing to this section of the state thirty-five years ago, Mr. and Mrs. Crossley took up their residence in the English Settlement.

The discovery of petroleum found Mr. Crossley's energetic spirit in the field of adventure, and he, together with Hon. William Barnsdall and Mr. P. T. Witherop, put down a well not far from the Drake well, in the winter of 1859–60, a few months after Colonel Drake had achieved his glorious success. Mr. Crossley's well was, of course, among the earliest drilled, and it gave to its owner the distinction of being among the real pioneer producers of oil, 200 barrels per day being the result of the new venture. This was a production thought to be marvelous in those days, and the excitement that was created spread far and wide, even attracting more attention in eastern cities than the Drake well. Mr. Crossley and his partners were "oil princes" then in the eyes of the hundreds of people who flocked into Titusville from all parts of the country.

This well, which to-day has a paying production, was followed by other enterprises of good, bad, and indifferent character, Mr. Crossley all the while showing a penchant for "wild-catting." He was associated with Messrs. Barnsdall and Watson in most of his operations. In his social and business habits Mr. Crossley was as near being exemplary as human nature could be. He was an upright, true conscientious man in every respect, and drew about him many valuable friends, who, beside his son and daughter, will deeply mourn his demise. His career as a Mason is prominent, being at the time of his death Past Master of Oil Creek Lodge AYM 308. This lodge was instituted Dec. 1st, 1856, and he was initiated as a member on the 22nd of the same month. He was Master in 1859 and again in 1864. This lodge will take charge of the funeral, which will be held in the Universalist church to-morrow morning at 10 o'clock. Mr. O. P. Kellogg, of Ohio, will officiate. The body will be buried with Masonic honors in the English Settlement cemetery.

Transcribed and shared on Find A Grave by contributor Robert Wheatall. Spelling and punctuation lightly regularized for readability.

One FamilySearch record gives David's birth year as 1818 (from a baptism dated 25 Jun 1820), a year later than the 1817 used throughout this page. The IGI record, Ancestry, and the arithmetic of his own obituary — which gives his age as 63 at his October 1880 death — all support 1817 instead; the 1818 figure is treated as a likely error in that single record.
David's emigration story exists in two different forms. His 1880 obituary, a secondhand recollection published at his death, says he was "brought to the United States by his grandmother." Two independently published, more detailed historical accounts — an 1898 mining history and a 1990 genealogy article — instead describe an eleven-year-old boy running away from home and crossing as a stowaway in 1828. Scott Morris's own working theory reconciles the two: David stowed away specifically aboard the same ship his grandmother Jane Inskip Harrison and her household were sailing on, running from home to join a crossing he wasn't officially part of, rather than boarding a separate vessel entirely. This would explain why the later obituary simplified the story to "brought by his grandmother," while the more detailed published accounts preserved the more dramatic reality. Unconfirmed pending a ship's manifest — stowaways, by nature, don't typically appear on official passenger lists, so proving this may depend on matching the sailing date and port to Jane's own documented household rather than finding David named directly.
The production figure for David's first oil well is given as 200 barrels a day in his 1880 obituary, but as 70–75 barrels a day in the more technical 1898 account in Sketches in Oil. Rather than a contradiction, both figures likely describe the same well at different points in its short life: the shallow, coarse producing sands common to this earliest phase of the Pennsylvania oil boom were known for a dramatic initial burst of production that quickly tapered off to a lower, steadier rate — the obituary's 200 barrels most likely reflects the well's brief early peak, and the 70–75 barrel figure its production once it settled.
