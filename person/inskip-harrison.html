Biography

Inskip Harrison was born 16 September 1812 in Lancashire, England, the youngest documented child of Benjamin Harrison and Jane Inskip Harrison — the same couple already documented on this site as founders of the Harrison family at the English Settlement. He was a younger brother to Richard Harrison, Edward Harrison, Elizabeth Harrison Gregory, Benjamin Inskip Harrison, and Jane Harrison Morris.

Inskip emigrated to the United States in 1826, at fourteen years old, and learned the stone-cutter's trade in New York City. His obituary does not say who he traveled with, but given his mother Jane Inskip Harrison's own documented emigration with her children, he most likely crossed as part of that same family group rather than alone. In 1833, at twenty-one, he walked to Pennsylvania — a documented ten-day journey made together with his brother Benjamin Inskip Harrison and his brother-in-law Richard Morris, husband to their sister Jane. The three men took up a single four-hundred-acre section of land and divided it into three lots, one for each family. Inskip lived with his aged mother, Jane Inskip Harrison, for many years afterward, and by his own obituary's account transformed his share of the land "from an almost unbroken wilderness into one of the finest farms in Crawford county." Of the three men who made that founding walk, Inskip was both the youngest — nine years younger than Benjamin Inskip Harrison and two years younger than Richard Morris — and the last to die, outliving both of his fellow founders by well over a decade.

On 20 March 1852, Inskip married Theresa Dailey at St. Matthew's Mission Church in Manhattan; her own page, already documented on this site, tells the fuller story of that marriage and the discrepancy between her stated age at the wedding and her later recorded birth year. The couple's son, William John Harrison, was born the following year in Philadelphia rather than Rome Township, suggesting the young family spent time in the city before eventually settling for good among the rest of the Harrisons. After Theresa's death in 1882, Inskip lived out his remaining years with William John at the English Settlement.

For more than twelve years, Inskip suffered from chronic kidney trouble, which his obituary says he bore "with Christian patience and resignation." He died early on the morning of Sunday, 25 November 1888, at seventy-six — the last survivor of the three men who had walked into the wilderness together fifty-five years before. His funeral was held the following Monday, 26 November, at two in the afternoon, at the United Presbyterian Church in the English Settlement. He was remembered, in his own obituary's words, as "a man highly esteemed by all who knew him," whose death was "considered a great loss in the community where he has so long resided." He was buried at the English Settlement, alongside the brother and brother-in-law who had made that first journey with him.

Obituary
Titusville Morning Herald · 27 November 1888 · p. 4
"Inskip Harrison — Early Sunday morning, November 25, 1888, there died at his residence, in the English Settlement, in this county, Mr. Inskip Harrison, at the age of 76 years. Mr. Harrison was born in Lancashire, England, September 16, 1812. He came to this country in 1826, when he was but 14 years old, and learned the stone-cutter's trade in New York city. In 1833, when he was 21 years of age, he walked to Pennsylvania and bought a farm in what is known as the English Settlement, where he lived with his aged mother for many years, and transformed his land from an almost unbroken wilderness into one of the finest farms in Crawford county. He was married in 1852 and, his wife dying in 1882, he has lived since then with his son, Mr. W. J. Harrison. For more than twelve years Mr. Harrison has suffered from kidney troubles and has borne his suffering with Christian patience and resignation. He was a man highly esteemed by all who knew him and his death is considered a great loss in the community where he has so long resided. His funeral took place on Monday, November 26, at 2 pm, at the United Presbyterian church in the English Settlement."

Settlement date — now reconciled, not just added to: The 1833 date given independently by both Inskip's obituary and the county history is well corroborated and specific (a documented ten-day walk, at a stated age of 21). It sits alongside three other candidate dates already flagged elsewhere on this site — 1834, 1837, and about 1845 — but unlike those, it plausibly reconciles rather than contradicts them: 1833 may mark the three men's own walk to establish and divide the land, with wives and children, including Richard and Jane Morris's own family, following later once married (1837 per Jane's son's memoir). This is offered as a reasoned synthesis, not a settled fact.
Acreage figure resolved in favor of 400: The county history's "four hundred acres... divided into three lots" matches the figure Scott Morris recalled from family memory, and supersedes Raymond Foley's "six hundred acres," flagged as a discrepancy on Jane Harrison Morris's page. Foley's figure is now treated as the likely error.
Two "Inskip"s in the family — do not conflate: Inskip Harrison (this page, 1812–1888) is distinct from his nephew Inskip Morris (1849–1930), son of Richard Morris and Jane Harrison Morris, who accompanied his mother on her 1860 trip to England. Both men are documented separately on this site.
Benjamin Inskip Harrison's ten children, per the county history: The same passage naming Inskip's 1833 walk states that Benjamin Inskip Harrison and Nancy Brown had ten children, naming as deceased (by the book's publication) "Jane, Sarah (first), Sarah (second), and Christopher," and as living "Benjamin, John, Betsy, Ellen, Richard, and Edward I." This suggests an early daughter named Sarah who died before the birth of the better-documented Sarah Harrison Harrison (1830–1887), and possibly a "Christopher" distinct from Richard Christopher Harrison (1838–1911), who was very much alive at the time of this book's publication despite being listed as deceased. Not resolved — worth revisiting when Benjamin Inskip Harrison's, Jane Harrison Morris (1818)'s, or Sarah Harrison Harrison's pages are next updated.
Northumberland vs. Yorkshire/Lancashire: The county history states Benjamin Inskip Harrison was born in Northumberland county, England — a different county from the Yorkshire and Lancashire origins established elsewhere on this site for the wider Harrison family. Not resolved; may reflect an error in the book, or a detail not yet reconciled with other sources.
1826 emigration companions not stated: Inskip's obituary gives his own arrival date and age (1826, 14 years old) but does not say who he traveled with. Given his mother Jane Inskip Harrison's own documented emigration with her children, he is treated here as most likely traveling with that family group rather than alone — but the obituary itself is silent on the point, so this remains an inference, not a documented fact.
Two different accounts of the journey itself: Raymond Foley's account (quoted on Jane Harrison Morris's page) describes the three families traveling by steamboat up the Hudson, canal boat to Buffalo, ship to Erie, and horse power into Rome Township. The county history and Inskip's own obituary instead describe the men walking the full distance, ten days on foot. Both cannot be literally true of the same trip; possibly Foley's account describes a different or later leg, or a family memory that shifted over time. Not resolved.
