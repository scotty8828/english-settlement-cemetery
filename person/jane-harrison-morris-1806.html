Biography

Early Life & The Loom

Born in Yorkshire, England, in 1806 to Benjamin Harrison and Jane Inskip Harrison, Jane moved to the industrial hub of Lancashire as an infant. There, she mastered the complex craft of textile weaving, operating power looms with six to eight treadles to produce fine, flowered fabrics. Following her father's death in 1824, twenty-year-old Jane emigrated to America alongside her widowed mother and her brothers, Benjamin and Inskip.

She quickly found highly regarded work in the booming textile centers of the Northeast, including Paterson (NJ), Fall River (MA), Columbiaville (NY), and Lowell (MA). In Lowell, Jane and an elite group of weavers were chosen to produce the finest "show cloth" of the era. It was during a snowy Massachusetts winter that Jane caused a local stir by wearing traditional English pattens — elevated wooden overshoes raised on oval iron rings. Churchgoers, unfamiliar with the strange iron tracks left in the snow on the church floor, briefly panicked, mistaking them for the footprints of the devil. Jane eventually returned to the New York area, where on 23 April 1830 she married fellow Lancashire emigrant Richard Morris at the Second Reformed Church in Wyckoff, New Jersey.

The Journey West & Settling Rome Township

Seeking a life beyond the industrial mills, Jane, Richard, and her brothers purchased a 400-acre tract of wilderness land from the Holland Land Company in Crawford County, Pennsylvania. Their journey west was a grueling expedition into a territory of virgin pine forest where roads were little more than underbrushed trails.

During the move, one of their horses fell ill, forcing the family to abandon their wagons and household goods in the deep woods overnight, just north of where the local settlers Bert Rowley and Ira Fish would later reside. While Richard sought help, Jane and her young son James sought shelter nearby, spending a cold frontier night under the hospitable roof of Hugh Coyle's home along the Titusville road. The next day, relatives arrived to haul their goods to the family's new homestead. Together, they established a prosperous farm and raised a large family. In total, Jane gave birth to eight children — seven sons and one daughter. Tragically, her only daughter and one infant son died while the family was still based in New York City, leaving six sons — Edward, James, William, Benjamin, John, and Inskip — to grow to adulthood on the Pennsylvania frontier.

Resilience Through War and Heritage

The American Civil War brought severe heartache to the Morris household. In 1862, against Richard's wishes, their second son, James, enlisted in a Pennsylvania volunteer regiment. He was mortally wounded on the front lines at Gettysburg in July 1863, later succumbing to his injuries at Fort Schuyler, New York. Without her husband's knowledge, Jane made secret, difficult arrangements to have James's body brought home from Fort Schuyler to Rome Township, where he was laid to rest in the family plot. His full story is told in a dedicated biography.

Throughout every hardship, Jane remained the anchoring heart of the family, later raising her grandson Fred after his mother, Lucy, died young. Driven by a deep pride in her family roots, Jane embarked on a four-month voyage back to England in May 1860, successfully tracing her lineage back several generations — though her "Yankee" sons often teased her about her pride in English aristocracy.

A Final Pioneer Act: The 1887 Cremation

Jane Harrison Morris passed away on 2 January 1887, at the age of 80. For the final ten years of her life, she harbored a profound fear of premature burial and left strict, unyielding instructions to be cremated — a practice virtually unknown in rural 19th-century Crawford County.

To honor her final wishes, her surviving sons consulted and cast lots to decide who would make the trip. The lot fell to William and Inskip. Dressed in a fine black silk dress, with her original 1830 wedding dress placed beside her in the casket because it no longer fit, Jane was escorted by her sons to the train station in Titusville. William and Inskip transported her remains to a crematory in Pittsburgh, managing the entire journey and process for a total cost of $70 to $75. Following the cremation, her ashes were returned to Rome Township and interred at English Settlement Cemetery alongside Richard. She is remembered as the first documented person in Crawford County history to be cremated.

Jane Harrison Morris is the confirmed link between the Harrison and Morris families at English Settlement — her marriage brought together two separately-emigrated Lancashire families, both of whom ended up settled and buried in the same small cemetery. The master cemetery record currently lists her as not buried here, which conflicts with the confirmed shared memorial with Richard Morris; this should be corrected in the data file. As with Richard's page, several children (Edward, Benjamin, John, Inskip) are not yet marked as buried here despite being documented family members, and William S. Morris has an unresolved date conflict with the existing "William S. Morris Jr." cemetery record — that record likely belongs to a different William Morris. Much of this biography's narrative detail (the loom work, the move west, the cremation) comes from two family memoirs written by descendants; specific dates and figures within them have not all been independently verified, though the marriage date and location are now confirmed by a primary church record.
