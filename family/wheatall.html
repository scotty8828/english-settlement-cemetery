The family story

London, and two brothers gone to sea

The Wheatall story begins with Benjamin Wheattall Sr. (c.1783–1858) and Iphigenia Dodd (c.1786–1851), Londoners who raised five children in the City of London — christened, one after another, at Saint Andrew Undershaft, a church better known as St Mary Axe. Two of those children died young in London: an infant daughter, also named Iphegenia, and a son, William Edward, who died at fourteen. The three who survived to adulthood scattered across the world in three different directions — one to rural Pennsylvania, one stayed rooted for decades in the same corner of that county, and one to Portugal.

Benjamin and Henry, the two surviving sons, were bound out to a ship's master as boys and became sailors, crossing the Atlantic together many times. Family memory holds that the brothers made a trip specifically to visit an old sailor they knew, named Stucky, who had settled in Rome Township, Crawford County. While they were there, their ship sailed without them. With nothing left to catch, they stayed — and both ended up, in their own ways, permanently part of this community.

Benjamin: a short life, an unexplained death

Benjamin J. Wheatall (1815–1845) is the reason this family has a place on this site at all — he's the only Wheatall actually buried at English Settlement Cemetery. Family memory holds he was courting Betsy Harrison (later Elizabeth Harrison Gilson, daughter of Benjamin Inskip Harrison) when he died suddenly, found dead in bed the morning after staying the night at her family's home. A 1979 transcription of the original 1845 coroner's inquest confirms it: ruled, in the language of the era, that he died "by the visitation of God in a natural way and not otherwise." He had bought 158 acres of his own land less than two months earlier. His full story, and the primary sources behind it, are on his own page.

Henry: the one who stayed for good

Henry Wheatall (1819–1891) is not buried at English Settlement Cemetery — he and his family rest at Union Cemetery in Titusville — but his life is the backbone of this family's American story, and Bob's research on him is extensive enough that it deserves telling here.

On Christmas Day 1845, just over three months after his brother's death, Henry married Elizabeth Harrison (1823–1883), daughter of Richard Harrison. His obituary in the Titusville Herald (5 June 1891) describes him plainly: "Mr. Wheatall was born in England, and in his younger days was a sailor. He followed that vocation for a number of years before coming to this country. He was one of the first settlers in this section, and lived about fifty years on the farm where he died. He was a good neighbor, honest and industrious... blessed with a cheerful disposition and invariably greeted his friends with a merry twinkle of the eye." After Elizabeth's death in 1883, Henry remarried twice more — to Mary A. Miller in 1885, and, controversially within the family, to a woman known as Mrs. M.M. Kerr in 1899, a marriage that ended in divorce a decade later.

Henry and Elizabeth had seven children. A daughter, Iphegenia — known as "Effie" — married Benjamin Morris, son of Richard Morris and Jane Harrison Morris, already well documented on this site; her stepson Fred Morris (from Benjamin's first marriage to Lucy Ann Sedden) was the same Fred raised by his grandmother Jane Harrison Morris after Lucy Ann's early death — a detail that connects this family directly into the Morris and Sedden threads already on this site. A son, Benjamin J. Wheatall (1846–1925, named for his late uncle), became a building contractor in Tonawanda, New York, and served as a justice of the peace there. Another son, Henry Richard Wheatall (1850–1930), worked the early Pennsylvania oil fields before returning to farming, and was married three times over his life.

The family also endured a devastating loss. In February 1873, three of Henry and Elizabeth's daughters — Eugenia (17), Lillian (14), and Adelaide (11) — died within eight days of one another. Their death records, compiled by Bob from Crawford County sources, give the cause plainly: a diphtheria epidemic. It's a stark reminder of how often disease could move through a single household in this era, taking multiple children in the span of days.

Phoebe: to Portugal, and where "Portuguese" came from

Benjamin and Henry's sister, Phoebe Wheattall (1822–1905), married John Fleurriet Delaforce, a member of the Delaforce family of Port wine merchants, and spent her life in Oporto, Portugal. A family tradition — recorded in a 1989 letter used elsewhere on this site — describes the Wheatalls as originally "a Portuguese family who had moved to England." Bob's research suggests this likely traces not to the Wheatalls themselves, who appear to have been Londoners for at least a generation before Benjamin and Henry, but to Phoebe's marriage into the Delaforce family — a connection so strong that a 1920 U.S. census taker, recording one of Henry's own grandsons, mistakenly wrote that his grandfather had been born in Portugal.
