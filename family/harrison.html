The family story

The Harrison name belongs to this settlement, but its roots trace back further still, through the family Jane Inskip Harrison was born into. The Inskips were yeoman farmers in the small hamlet of Smalden, near Bolton by Bowland, on the Yorkshire side of the Lancashire border — land records in the Slaidburn Court Rolls show the family holding tenancy there as far back as 1669, when it passed from Thomas Inskip to his son Richard. For well over a century before Jane's own birth, land at Smalden moved from father to son in the same customary way: on the father's death, or when the son turned 21, whichever came first.

The Inskips were also Nonconformists — dissenters from the Church of England — a meaningful identity in rural Yorkshire at the time. Jane's father, Richard Inskip (1724–1807), was a trustee of the Independent Chapel at Holden, near Bolton by Bowland, and three of his daughters were baptized there not by a settled vicar but by a travelling minister, Peter Walkden, whose diary and chapel registers survive and have since been published. It's a small detail that says something real about the world Jane was born into: rural, dispersed, and shaped by a faith practiced outside the established church. The surname Inskip itself likely traces to a village of the same name near Preston, Lancashire — the family's name, like its land, tied to a specific patch of English ground for generations before any of them ever considered leaving it.

Origins research above draws on Craig Thornber's compiled Inskip family history (thornber.net), itself built from Lancashire parish and probate records, the Slaidburn Court Rolls, and contributions from several researchers including Bob Wheatall and Reg Postlethwaite.

The Harrison family's story as it relates to this cemetery starts with Jane Inskip Harrison (1766–1845), born in Yorkshire and married to Benjamin Harrison in Bolton-by-Bowland in 1784. The family later settled in Lancashire's textile district, where Jane became a skilled handloom weaver. After Benjamin's death in 1824, Jane — then in her sixties — gathered seven of her nine children and emigrated to America in 1827, settling first in Paterson, New Jersey. A small grandson traveled with her too: David Crossley, son of her daughter Anne, carried across the Atlantic by his grandmother rather than his own parents — the beginning of a family thread that connects the Harrisons directly to the Crossley family also documented on this site.

In 1833, Jane's sons Benjamin Inskip Harrison and Inskip Harrison, together with her son-in-law Richard Morris, purchased 400 acres in Rome Township, Crawford County, and moved the family onto the Pennsylvania frontier — founding what became known as the English Settlement, alongside Richard Harrison, Jane's eldest son. Jane's daughter-in-law Nancy Brown Harrison, married to Benjamin Inskip Harrison, was one of three women who made that same founding journey, alongside Jane and her daughter Jane Harrison Morris.

From there the family grew quickly, and intermarried repeatedly with the other Lancashire families who settled nearby — the Morrises, the Seddens, the Wheatalls. Common names recur constantly across generations (Benjamin, Jane, Inskip, Richard, Elizabeth), which has made this family, more than any other on this site, a real exercise in not confusing one relative for another.

This page is a starting point, not a finished history. As more individual Harrison biographies are built, they'll be added below — the goal is for this page to grow naturally alongside the rest of the project, rather than becoming a separate undertaking later.
